## QWERTY vs Dvorak vs Colemak vs Workman

A keyboard layout is just the map of which letter sits under each key. QWERTY has been the default since the 1870s, but alternative layouts rearrange the letters so the most common ones sit on the home row, cutting finger travel. Here is how the four best-known layouts compare on the same English text.

Layout | Introduced | Finger travel* | Home-row use | Same-finger
QWERTY | 1870s | 153 m (baseline) | 36% | 7.9%
Dvorak | 1936 | 88 m (&minus;42%) | 71% | 3.2%
Colemak | 2006 | 89 m (&minus;42%) | 73% | 1.5%
Workman | 2010 | 84 m (&minus;45%) | 67% | 2.0%

*Modeled finger travel for the same 5,847-character English passage on a standard keyboard, with the share of letters typed without leaving the home row and the share of consecutive keys pressed by the same finger. Figures from the Typing Cat layout comparison lab; they are estimates of movement, not measured typing speeds.

## Which keyboard layout is best?

There is no single best layout, because less finger movement is not the same as faster typing. QWERTY wins on familiarity: it is on every shared computer and every shortcut assumes it. Dvorak alternates hands the most (about 67% of keystrokes), which many typists find rhythmic for long English prose. Colemak keeps the most letters on the home row (73%) and the fewest awkward same-finger presses (1.5%) while leaving Z, X, C, V and most shortcuts in place, so it is the usual pick for QWERTY users who want to switch. Workman trims total finger travel the most by also minimising sideways stretches.

For almost everyone, practice and accuracy move your WPM far more than the layout does. A fast QWERTY typist will beat a beginner on any layout. Switch for comfort and reduced finger travel, not for a guaranteed speed jump.

## Should you switch keyboard layout?

Switching costs you one to four weeks of slower, deliberate typing while new muscle memory forms, and shared or public machines will still be QWERTY. The payoff is less finger travel and, for many people, more comfort in long sessions. It is worth it mainly if you type a lot of English every day and own the keyboards you use most.

If you want the gentlest change, start with Colemak because it keeps your common shortcuts. If you love hand-alternation, try Dvorak. The honest answer for casual typists is that staying on QWERTY and practising is the higher-return move.

## Keyboard layouts FAQ

### Which keyboard layout is the fastest?

No layout is reliably fastest for everyone. Modeling shows Dvorak, Colemak and Workman cut finger travel by roughly 42 to 45% versus QWERTY, but measured typing speed depends far more on practice and accuracy than on the layout. The world's fastest typists use several different layouts, including QWERTY.

### Is Dvorak better than QWERTY?

Dvorak puts about 71% of letters on the home row versus QWERTY's 36% and alternates hands more, which many typists find more comfortable for long English text. Studies show modest speed gains at best, so the main benefit is reduced finger movement and comfort, not a guaranteed speed jump.

### Is Colemak better than Dvorak?

Colemak keeps more letters on the home row (about 73%) and the lowest same-finger rate (about 1.5%), and it changes only 17 keys while keeping Z, X, C and V in their QWERTY places, so it is easier to switch to than Dvorak. Dvorak alternates hands more. Which feels better is personal.

### What is the best keyboard layout for programming?

Code uses far more punctuation, brackets and symbols than English prose, so prose-optimised layouts help less. Programmer variants such as Programmer Dvorak and Workman Programmer rearrange the number and symbol rows. Many developers stay on QWERTY because editors, shortcuts and pair-programming all assume it.

### How long does it take to switch keyboard layouts?

Most people reach usable speed again in one to four weeks of short daily practice, and return to their old speed over one to three months. Accuracy recovers before raw speed, so track accuracy first.
