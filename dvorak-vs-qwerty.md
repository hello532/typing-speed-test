# Dvorak vs QWERTY

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/dvorak-vs-qwerty.html

Dvorak vs QWERTY is typing's oldest argument. One side promises efficiency, the other points at a century of inertia. Here is the honest version: what each layout is, what the evidence says, and who should actually switch.

## How the two layouts differ

QWERTY dates to the 1870s typewriter era, with common letters scattered across all rows. Dvorak, designed in 1936, concentrates the most-used letters on the home row: about 70% of English typing happens there versus roughly 30% on QWERTY. The design goals were less finger travel, more alternation between hands, and fewer awkward reaches.

The layouts are not different in kind — both are letter arrangements for the same fingers. They differ in load distribution: Dvorak shifts work toward the home row and the stronger middle and index fingers, QWERTY spreads it more thinly across rows and weaker fingers.

## What the speed evidence actually says

The honest summary of decades of claims and studies: Dvorak users report comfort improvements more consistently than speed improvements. Controlled comparisons generally find modest speed differences — a few percent in either direction — swamped by the variation between individual typists. Several modern speed-record holders do use Dvorak, but they would be elite on any layout.

What is well supported: the learning investment. Retraining from fluent QWERTY to functional Dvorak takes roughly one to three months of daily practice, with a painful dip where you type slower than before. The break-even point on that investment is measured in years, not weeks.

## The comfort argument, which is stronger

Where Dvorak has the better case is ergonomics: less finger travel and more home-row work means less cumulative movement, which many users experience as reduced fatigue and strain over long sessions. If you type for hours daily and your hands hurt, layout is a legitimate variable to experiment with.

Colemak, a third option, keeps QWERTY's familiar positions for most keys (and shortcuts) while moving the most common letters home-row-ward. It offers much of Dvorak's comfort benefit with a shorter retraining curve, which is why it has become the pragmatic middle choice for many switchers.

## The hidden costs of switching

Beyond retraining time, switching has practical taxes: shared computers become awkward (everyone else's machine is QWERTY), keyboard shortcuts move (Ctrl+C/V sit in new places), and mobile keyboards stay QWERTY regardless. You are effectively committing to being bilingual in layouts, or to friction every time you touch someone else's device.

There is also the measurement trap: your QWERTY speed feels like 'your speed', so Dvorak's slow phase feels like regression. Most switchers quit in week two or three, before the curve turns. Those who persist past month one almost never switch back — the two facts together tell you most of what you need to know.

## Who should switch, and who should not

Switch if: you are a heavy daily typist with hand or wrist discomfort, you are learning from scratch (no QWERTY habits to unlearn), or you are simply curious and accept the two-to-three-month cost. Do not switch if: you type an hour or two a day without pain, you need speed this quarter for a test or job, or you frequently use other people's computers.

For the vast majority of typists, the better investment is technique on QWERTY: home-row discipline, accuracy-first practice and weak-key elimination deliver larger gains than any layout change, at zero retraining cost.

## Try before you commit

The sensible experiment: keep QWERTY for daily work and practise Dvorak twenty minutes a day for a month. If the curve excites you, switch fully; if it frustrates you, you lost nothing but a few hours. The Dvorak typing test lets you feel the layout right now, in your browser, with nothing to install.

Measure honestly through the experiment: weekly scores on both layouts, same text type. Your own numbers beat any forum argument — including this article's.

## The verdict in one paragraph

Dvorak is a comfort upgrade with a real but modest efficiency argument and a heavy entry fee; QWERTY is the entrenched standard whose main advantage is that you already know it. Neither layout makes you fast; technique does. If you have pain or curiosity, try Dvorak properly for a month. Otherwise, put the months into accuracy and weak-key work on QWERTY — the returns are bigger and arrive sooner.

## The history that explains the inertia

QWERTY won before ergonomics was a word. Its layout settled in the 1870s and froze into every typewriter, terminal and keyboard since — an accident of history upgraded to a standard. By the time Dvorak published his research in 1936, millions of trained typists already existed, and retraining them was never economically interesting.

This is the pattern to recognise in any layout debate: standards win through installed base, not superiority. It does not make QWERTY good — it makes QWERTY free, because the world already taught it to you.

## Dvorak for programmers and symbol-heavy work

One group deserves a specific note: programmers. Code typing is dominated by symbols — brackets, semicolons, equals — whose positions differ between layouts, and some coders find Dvorak's symbol placement worse than QWERTY's for their specific language. If you type code all day, test the layout on actual code before committing, not just on prose.

## Making the final decision

Summarise the whole question into one test: does your current typing hurt, or does layout fascinate you enough to pay two months of slow typing? ## Dvorak vs QWERTY FAQ

### Is Dvorak really faster than QWERTY?

The evidence shows modest differences at best — a few percent either way, dwarfed by individual variation. Dvorak's stronger case is comfort: ~70% of typing on the home row means less finger travel and often less fatigue.

### How long does it take to learn Dvorak?

One to three months of daily practice to functional speed for a fluent QWERTY typist, with a frustrating slow phase in weeks two and three. Learning from scratch skips the unlearning cost entirely.

### Should I switch from QWERTY to Dvorak?

Only if you type heavily every day with discomfort, or you are starting from zero. For most people, technique work on QWERTY (home row, accuracy-first, weak keys) delivers bigger gains with no retraining cost.

### What about Colemak?

Colemak keeps most QWERTY key positions and shortcuts while moving common letters to the home row — much of Dvorak's comfort benefit with a shorter retraining curve. A pragmatic middle option for switch-curious typists.
