# Audio Typing Tips

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/audio-typing-tips.html

Audio typing tips are different from typing tips: in transcription the enemy is not the keyboard, it is the audio that keeps playing while you are still typing the last sentence. These skills close that gap.

## Build the listen-type pipeline

Professional audio typists hold three to five seconds of speech in memory while their fingers finish the previous phrase — listening ahead and typing behind simultaneously. This pipeline is the core skill of transcription, and it is trainable: start by typing along to any clear podcast, pausing freely at first, then reducing pauses to zero over about two weeks.

The pipeline breaks when you try to type word-for-word in real time. Let yourself lag deliberately; a controlled three-second lag is smoother and faster than constant catch-up panic.

## Chunk the audio, not the keyboard

Work in meaning chunks, not words. Listen for a full phrase, hold it, type it, and use the natural pause between phrases to catch up. Short rewind is your friend: replaying a phrase once is always faster than guessing wrong and fixing it in proofreading.

Playback speed control matters more than most beginners realise. Most tools let you run audio at 80–90% speed; that small reduction cuts errors dramatically while barely costing time. Speed the audio up only after your accuracy holds at normal pace for a full project.

## Let text expansion carry the boilerplate

Transcription is full of repeated phrases — speaker labels, common openers, domain terms. A text expander turns 'int' into 'interviewer' or a full speaker tag with two keystrokes, and over an hour of audio it saves hundreds of keystrokes and, more importantly, keeps your rhythm unbroken.

Build your expansion list from your last project: anything you typed five times or more is a candidate. Review it monthly; the list compounds into a personal shorthand that no generic tool ships with.

## Accuracy habits that actually pay

Transcription pays for clean text, so: never guess a word silently — mark it [unclear] per the style guide and move on; a marked guess costs nothing, a wrong word can cost the contract. Proofread once against the audio at 1.5x speed, hunting for meaning errors rather than typos. And keep your typing accuracy trained separately on the typing accuracy test — audio skills and keyboard accuracy compound independently.

For speed foundations, the CPM typing test tracks the metric transcription is paid on, and transcription typing jobs covers qualifying for the work itself.

## Equipment that changes the work

Three purchases define an audio typist's kit. Closed-back headphones: isolation reveals mumbled words that speakers hide, and they protect your hearing over long sessions. A foot pedal: hands-free play, pause and rewind keeps both hands on the keyboard — the single biggest throughput upgrade in transcription. And a proper media player with speed control and hotkeys, because rewinding with a mouse breaks rhythm every time.

Buy in that order as budget allows. None of them is required to start — clear audio and free players exist — but each one pays for itself in hours saved within weeks.

## Handling accents and fast speakers

Accents stop being a problem when you stop fighting them word by word. The technique: listen to the first minute without typing anything, just calibrating to the speaker's rhythm and vowel patterns. Your brain adapts remarkably fast — the same voice that was unintelligible at second ten is clear by minute two.

Fast speakers yield to the speed control: run them at 80% pace and they become ordinary. The mistake is trying to keep up at full speed and producing error-dense text that takes longer to fix than the slowdown costs.

## Research skills: names, terms and places

Every transcript contains names, technical terms and places you cannot reliably guess from audio. Professional habit: mark them on first pass, then research in a dedicated pass — search the project context, speaker names, organisation websites, spelling variants. Five minutes of research per hour of audio is normal and expected.

Keep a project glossary as you go: confirmed spellings of every name and term. It halves research time on long projects and it is the difference between a transcript that reads professionally and one that reads guessed.

## Timestamping and formatting discipline

Most style guides require timestamps at intervals or at every unclear passage, plus consistent speaker labels. The efficient method: insert timestamps and labels as you go with hotkeys or expander shortcuts — retrofitting them in proofing takes twice as long.

Formatting errors are the most avoidable reason transcripts get rejected. Keep the style guide open, build your expander entries for its exact formats, and let muscle memory handle compliance while you focus on the audio.

## Proofing against the audio, not the text

The final quality gate is a proofing pass, and the rule is: proof against the audio, not against your text. Reading your own transcript silently shows you what you meant to type; listening again shows you what the speaker actually said. Meaning errors — wrong word, dropped negation — only surface against the audio.

Run the proof pass at 1.5x speed, hunting meaning rather than commas. One focused pass catches what matters; three unfocused passes catch less and cost your evening.

A last habit from veterans: end every workday with two minutes of log — files done, audio minutes, error notes. It takes almost no time, and it turns vague feelings of a good or bad week into data you can actually improve from.

In a trade paid by the audio minute, knowing your own numbers is the difference between guessing your rate and setting it.

## Audio Typing Tips FAQ

### How can I get faster at audio typing?

Train the listen-type pipeline: type along to a clear podcast, pausing freely at first, then reduce pauses to zero over two weeks. Work in phrase chunks, not words, and run audio at 80-90% speed until accuracy holds.

### What is the listen-type pipeline?

Holding 3-5 seconds of speech in memory while your fingers finish the previous phrase — listening ahead and typing behind at once. It is the core transcription skill and it is trainable with real audio practice.

### Should I use text expansion for transcription?

Yes — speaker labels and repeated phrases are boilerplate. Expanders save hundreds of keystrokes per hour and, more importantly, keep your rhythm unbroken. Build the list from anything you typed 5+ times in the last project.

### What do I do when I cannot hear a word?

Mark it [unclear] per the style guide and move on — never guess silently. Rewind once for a second listen; if it is still unclear, mark it. A marked gap costs nothing; a wrong word can cost the contract.
