## 1 minute typing test

A 1 minute typing test is the standard way to measure typing speed: you type a passage for exactly sixty seconds, then read your score in words per minute (WPM) and accuracy. One minute is long enough to smooth out a lucky or unlucky burst, and short enough that you can retake it every day without thinking about it. Most schools, employers and online benchmarks quote speed from a sixty-second run, which makes this the most comparable single measurement in typing.

## How the one minute test works

Select the 1 min duration, click the text area and start typing — the timer starts on your first keystroke, not before, so you can settle your hands first. While you type you see the live countdown, your current WPM and your accuracy. When the clock reaches zero the test locks and shows the full result: net WPM, raw WPM, accuracy, characters per minute, errors and a consistency score.

Net WPM is the number that matters: it is your gross speed with errors deducted, so a fast but sloppy run scores lower than a slightly slower clean one. Raw WPM ignores errors and shows your pure finger speed. The gap between the two tells you whether to push speed or tighten accuracy. After the result you also get a per-key report — the letters you miss most and the letters that slow you down — which turns one sixty-second run into a practice plan.

## One minute vs other test lengths

Every duration answers a different question. Short sprints measure peak speed, the classic sixty seconds measures your working speed, and the long formats expose what fatigue does to your accuracy. The table below shows what each length on this site actually tells you and when to use it.

Duration | What it measures | Best for
15 seconds | Peak burst speed before fatigue | Warm-up, quick daily check
30 seconds | Burst speed with a little settling | Tracking sprint progress
1 minute | Working speed — the standard benchmark | Résumés, school, daily measurement
2 minutes | Speed sustained past the first adrenaline | Checking if your 60s score holds up
3 minutes | Early fatigue effects on accuracy | Exam-style practice
5 minutes | Real stamina and concentration | Data-entry and transcription work
10 minutes | Full endurance typing | Long-form writers, professionals

A useful rule: if your two-minute score is much lower than your sixty-second score, your speed is real but your consistency is not — practise accuracy, not sprints. If the two scores are close, your technique is stable and you can safely push for more speed.

## What your 1 minute score means

Typing speed falls into rough bands. Around 40 WPM is the average adult — fine for everyday email and documents. Between 60 and 80 WPM beats most office workers and is the usual 'fast typist' claim on a résumé. Above 80 WPM is professional territory, and anything past 100 WPM puts you in roughly the top one percent of typists.

Accuracy matters as much as the headline number. A 55 WPM run at 98% accuracy is worth more to an employer than 70 WPM at 90%, because error correction is the hidden tax on every document you produce. Aim for 97% accuracy or better first; speed follows on its own.

## Reading your 1 minute result in detail

The headline number is net WPM: correct characters typed, divided by five, divided by the time — the industry-standard formula, with errors already deducted. Next to it sits raw WPM, your gross speed before any penalty. When the gap between the two is small, your technique is clean and speed work will pay off. When the gap is wide, every extra word of speed is being bought with mistakes you then pay back in corrections.

The consistency percentage measures the evenness of your keystroke timing. Two typists can finish with the same net score while one typed in smooth rhythm and the other in bursts and stalls; the smooth typist gets the higher consistency, and in real work the smooth typist tires less and errors less. Below the scores, the per-key report lists your most-missed letters and your slowest letters — they are rarely the same key. Missed keys need accuracy drills; slow keys need repetition until the reach becomes automatic.

Every result is saved privately in your browser, so the one-minute run doubles as a progress log. Comparing today's score with last week's tells you more than any single run, because daily scores wander by a few points in both directions — the trend line is the real measurement.

## How to get a higher 1 minute score

Warm up with a 15-second sprint, then take the full minute seriously — your second run of the day is usually your best. Keep your eyes on the screen and your fingers on the home row; every glance at the keyboard costs you several words over sixty seconds. Type slightly below your panic speed: the single biggest score killer is the error-and-backspace cycle.

Train the text types that slow you down. If numbers and punctuation break your rhythm, run a few rounds in numbers or quotes mode until they feel automatic — symbols are the most common slowdown in any timed run. Use the weak-key report after each test and drill those letters specifically instead of re-typing whole passages you already handle well. Ten focused minutes a day beats an occasional hour.

## Five mistakes that cap your 1 minute score

Typing at panic speed. Pushing past the point where your accuracy holds, then donating the gains back through corrections. The fix feels backwards but works: slow down five percent and the net score climbs.
Looking at the keyboard. Every glance breaks rhythm and costs several words over sixty seconds. If you still need to look, spend a week on home-row drills before chasing speed.
Ignoring symbols. Digits and punctuation are where otherwise-fast typists lose the most time, because practice usually means plain prose. A few rounds a week in numbers or quotes mode closes that gap quickly.
Retaking immediately without reading the weak-key report. You rehearse the same errors and they set deeper. Read the report, drill the two worst keys, then retest.
Practising in rare long sessions. Typing skill is motor memory, and motor memory consolidates with daily repetition. Ten focused minutes every day moves the score more than an occasional hour.

## A simple 7-day plan for a higher 1 minute score

Day one: take the test once, note your net WPM and accuracy, and read the weak-key report carefully — that is your baseline. Day two: accuracy day. Type slower than feels natural and finish above 98%; the number does not matter today. Day three: symbols day, in numbers and quotes modes, because symbols are the most common rhythm-breaker.

Day four: weak-key day. Take the two keys the report flagged and drill them with short 15-second rounds until the reach is automatic. Day five: speed day — warm up with two 15-second sprints, then give the full minute everything you have; sprint-warmed fingers reliably score higher. Day six: stamina day, one two-minute run. If it lands far below your sixty-second score, consistency — not speed — is what to train next.

Day seven: rest your hands, then retake the baseline under the same conditions as day one — same time of day, same keyboard, same warm-up. Compare net WPM, accuracy and consistency side by side. Most people see a real gain in the first week, almost all of it coming from accuracy rather than raw speed. Repeat the cycle and let the trend line compound.

## Setting up a fair 1 minute test

Scores are only comparable when conditions are stable. Test at the same time of day — most people type noticeably faster mid-morning than late at night — and on the same keyboard, because key travel and layout differences change your numbers more than a week of practice does. Sit with both feet on the floor, wrists slightly raised, and close anything that might interrupt you mid-run.

Warm up with a single 15-second sprint before the measured run; cold fingers underperform by several points and make your history noisy. Then settle, breathe, and start typing — the timer waits for your first keystroke, so there is no reason to rush the start.

## Why sixty seconds became the standard

The one-minute format won out because it sits at a sweet spot between stability and effort. Anything shorter — ten or fifteen seconds — measures your burst speed, which flattered by adrenaline and a lucky first word; anything longer starts measuring fatigue as much as skill. Sixty seconds is long enough for the lucky and unlucky moments to average out, so two runs on different days produce genuinely comparable numbers, yet short enough that retaking it daily costs nothing.

That comparability is why schools, employers and typing benchmarks settled on the minute as the default unit of account. When a job posting says 'types 60 WPM', it almost always means sixty WPM measured over a one-minute passage at high accuracy. Practising in exactly that format means the number you quote and the number they test are the same number.

There is a practical benefit for training too. Because a run costs only a minute, it survives as a habit: before work, after lunch, between tasks. Skill in typing compounds through exactly that kind of frequent, measurable repetition, and no other duration makes measurement this cheap.

## What to do after a one minute test

A single number is not progress. Log the score, then use the typing practice plan for a week and retake the same length under the same conditions. If the score moves, keep the routine; if it stalls, the how to get better at typing guide explains which drill matches which bottleneck.

## Typing test FAQ

### What is a 1 minute typing test?

A 60-second timed round: type the passage shown for exactly one minute, then get your WPM, accuracy and error breakdown instantly. One minute is the most-quoted test length, so scores compare directly across sites and job requirements.

### How is WPM scored on the 1 minute test?

Correct characters divided by 5, divided by the one elapsed minute. Because the test lasts exactly sixty seconds the maths is simple: 300 correct characters equals 60 WPM. The results panel shows both raw WPM and net WPM with errors subtracted.

### Is 1 minute the right test length?

For most people, yes: 60 seconds balances speed bursts against fatigue and matches the length employers and benchmarks use. Take longer 3 or 5 minute tests when you want a steadier, hiring-grade number.

### Is the 1 minute test free?

Yes — unlimited 1 minute rounds, no sign up, no ads in the typing area. Your history stays in your own browser.

### How can I raise my 1 minute score?

Warm up with one throwaway round, then focus on accuracy: at 95%+ accuracy speed follows. Keep your fingers on the home row, look at the text not your hands, and practise daily with typing drills.

### Is a 1 minute typing test accurate?

Yes, for a quick check. Sixty seconds is long enough that one lucky sentence does not decide the result, and it is the length employers and typing certificates use, so your score compares directly. Take a 3 or 5 minute test when you want a steadier number.
