# Typing Test for Court Reporters

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/typing-test-for-court-reporter.html

Court reporting certification is 225 words per minute — on a stenotype machine, not a QWERTY keyboard. The two numbers are not comparable, and confusing them is the most common mistake applicants make.

A stenotype writes syllables, not letters: multiple keys are pressed together and a chord resolves into a word or phrase. That is how 225 WPM is reachable at all. A QWERTY keyboard writes letter by letter, and the fastest competitive typists in the world top out around 200 WPM in short bursts on text they can see.

So the 225 WPM requirement is not a claim that court reporters can type 225 WPM on a laptop. It is a test of realtime dictation capture through a machine built for it.

Speed | Machine | Typical meaning
180 WPM | Stenotype | Often the floor for graduation from a reporting program
200 WPM | Stenotype | Entry-level freelance work in many markets
225 WPM | Stenotype | The certified reporter standard, tested on live dictation
260 WPM | Voice or stenotype | Higher-tier certification for fast proceedings
60–90 WPM | QWERTY | Transcript editing, scoping, proofreading support roles

The lower bands are programme requirements rather than universal law: schools and states vary. Check the specific certification body before planning a timeline around one number.

Most working hours are not spent on the machine. They are spent editing: correcting names, fixing speaker attribution, verifying exhibit numbers and formatting the transcript. That work happens on a keyboard, and it is where a reporter's day actually goes.

Scoping and proofreading roles, which are the usual entry point while certification is pending, are keyboard jobs outright. A 60–80 WPM editing speed with clean accuracy is the practical requirement.

Dictation practice, not typing practice, moves the stenotype number: five minutes at a speed where you can write every word, then a speed where you cannot. The second set is where the growth happens, provided you review the strokes you fumbled.

Use a keyboard test for the editing half of the job. Ten minutes of 10-minute rounds at 98% accuracy will make transcript cleanup painless, and it is the part candidates neglect because it is not the number they are chasing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do court reporters really type 225 WPM on a keyboard?

No. 225 WPM is a stenotype standard, achieved by chording syllables on a purpose-built machine. Keyboard typing at that speed is not what the certification measures.

### What is a good QWERTY speed for a court reporter?

60–90 WPM is comfortable for transcript editing, scoping and proofreading, which is where most keyboard time goes. Accuracy matters more than speed because transcript corrections are billable work.

### How long does it take to reach 225 WPM?

Reporting programmes typically run two to four years, and speed is the usual reason people take longer. The machine skill is built by daily dictation practice at the edge of your current speed.

### Can I become a court reporter without a stenotype?

There are voice-writing and digital reporting routes, and some jurisdictions accept them. They have their own certification requirements, so confirm with the body that governs your state or country.

### Is a 10-minute typing test relevant?

For the editing side, yes — it measures sustained keyboard accuracy rather than a burst. It will not predict your steno speed, which is a different motor skill.
