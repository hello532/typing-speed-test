# Bigram Typing Practice

A bigram typing practice drill targeting the most common English letter combinations.

## What is bigram typing practice?

A bigram is a pair of consecutive letters that appears very often in English: th, he, in, er, on, ed, nd, ti, es, re, ou, ar and st. These thirteen pairs dominate English text, so a bigram typing practice drill that repeats them builds the exact finger coordination that real-world typing demands.

When the letters th appear in a word, your two fingers must coordinate - one strikes t while the other prepares h. If this transition is hesitant, every word containing th is slowed. Drill the bigram and every matching word speeds up.

## Common vs rare bigrams

There are two drill modes. Common bigrams (th, he, in, er, on) train the transitions you use every minute. Rare bigrams (wh, kn, gh, qu, ch, ght, ugh, ought) train the combinations that break rhythm even in skilled typists. Start with common until they feel automatic, then switch to rare for a challenge.

Alternating between the two keeps each round fresh while covering the full range of English letter combinations.

Practice common bigrams until automatic, then hard bigrams until comfortable, and finally full sentences to tie them together. ## Rare bigrams that break rhythm

While common bigrams build your baseline, rare bigrams are what hold elite typists back from the next level. The combinations wh, kn, gh, qu, ch and the clusters ght, ugh, ought appear in words like though, knight, bought, through and thought. These are high-frequency in real text but rare in typical practice, so your fingers never build the automatic reach for them.

The rare bigram mode on this page packs every round with these combinations at high density. A few 15-second sessions daily will train these alternate reaches until they feel as natural as the home row.

## How bigram practice translates to real text

The most common objection to bigram practice is that real text is full sentences, not random pairs. This has a clear answer: when you type a sentence like 'the thing that made them start', your fingers fire a chain of bigrams: th, em, ad, ar, rt. Each pair has been drilled individually. The sentence becomes a smooth sequence of known patterns rather than a series of new decisions.

Research on motor learning calls this chunking: the brain groups repeated movements into single units. Bigram typing practice is chunking at the letter-pair level.

## How bigram fluency compounds

Every English word is built from letter combinations, and the same combinations repeat across related words. When you drill th in the words the, that, this and three, you are also training the th in their, them, other and weather. Bigram practice compounds across vocabulary because the alphabet only has 26 letters and English only has a handful of high-frequency pairs.
