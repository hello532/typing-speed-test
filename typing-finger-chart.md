# Typing Finger Chart

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/typing-finger-chart.html

A typing finger chart assigns every key to a specific finger — it is the map that makes blind typing possible. Here is the full assignment explained, how to learn it in a week, and the mistakes that make people re-learn it later.

## The complete finger assignment

Left pinky: Q, A, Z, plus left shift, tab, caps and control keys. Left ring: W, S, X. Left middle: E, D, C. Left index: R, F, V and T, G, B — two columns, which is why the index is the workhorse. Right index: Y, H, N and U, J, M. Right middle: I, K, comma. Right ring: O, L, period. Right pinky: P, semicolon, slash, plus right shift and enter. Both thumbs share the spacebar.

Notice the logic: stronger fingers carry more keys, the home row (A-S-D-F / J-K-L-;) is the base every finger returns to, and columns slant diagonally down-left from the home position. The chart is not arbitrary — it is the shortest-reach assignment for a standard keyboard.

## Why the index fingers get two columns

The index fingers are the strongest and most dextrous, so the chart doubles their territory: left index covers F-home plus R-T-G-V-B, right index covers J-home plus Y-U-H-N-M. This double duty is also the most common place discipline breaks — the index loves to 'help' other fingers and wreck the whole map.

Keep the index in its own territory. Every time it steals a key from a weaker finger, that finger gets weaker practice and the chart blurs in your muscle memory.

## How to learn the chart in a week

Day one and two: home row only, eyes on screen, until the eight home keys are reflexive. Day three and four: add the top row reaches, each finger striking up into its column and snapping back home. Day five and six: add the bottom row, the hardest reaches — slow down and let accuracy lead.

Day seven: full-chart sentences, slowly, with the cover-the-keyboard trick if your eyes keep cheating. One focused week of ten-minute daily sessions builds the whole map; the month after makes it automatic.

## The three assignment mistakes everyone makes

Mistake one: pressing space with the index finger instead of the thumb — a hunt-and-peck relic that breaks right-hand home position on every space. Mistake two: using the wrong shift — capitalising a left-hand letter with left shift, tying your hand in a knot; use the opposite-side shift always. Mistake three: index finger drifting to another finger's column, especially for C, E, I and M.

Each mistake has the same fix: catch it once, drill that exact reach with the correct finger in 15-second bursts for a few days. The chart you practise is the chart you keep.

## The chart for numbers and symbols

Number row follows the same column logic stretched one row up: left pinky reaches 1 and backtick, through right pinky on 0, minus and equals. It is a longer reach, which is exactly why digits break rhythm — they demand chart discipline at the edge of comfortable range.

Symbols ride the number keys with shift (opposite-side shift, always). If your work is symbol-heavy — code, data entry — dedicated numbers-mode practice pays for itself in days.

## Making the chart automatic

A chart on the wall teaches nothing by itself; the assignment becomes automatic only through correct repetition. Ten minutes daily with eyes on the screen, errors ignored, correct fingers enforced — that is the entire method. Speed is forbidden until the map is reflexive; speed with wrong fingers is just faster wrongness.

Test your reflexes weekly on the keyboard practice page and the home row typing test: if you can hit every key blind without a pause to think 'which finger', the chart has moved from paper to muscle.

## Print or memorise?

Print the chart for the first three days — glancing at a reference beside the screen is legal and useful; glancing at the keyboard is not. Then take it away. The goal is spatial memory, and spatial memory only forms when the eyes are denied the answer.

Within a week you will notice your fingers 'know' reaches your brain could not describe. That is the chart fully learned: not memorised, installed.

## The chart for the numeric keypad

The keypad has its own mini-chart: index finger on 4, middle on 5 (home bump), ring on 6, thumb on 0, and the pinky or ring covering enter and plus. Home base is the 4-5-6 row, and every reach returns there — the same snap-back logic as the main keyboard in miniature.

If your work involves volume numbers — data entry, accounting, spreadsheets — learn this chart as a separate week of practice. The 10 key typing test trains it directly.

## Common chart variations you may meet

Some systems swap minor assignments: certain charts give the B key to the left index (standard) while others debate it; split and ergonomic keyboards redraw columns entirely; and alternative layouts like Dvorak and Colemak keep the same finger logic on new letter positions.

The invariants survive every variation: home base exists, each finger owns territory, and every reach returns home. Learn the invariants once and any chart you meet later is just a re-skin.

## Teaching the chart to someone else

The chart is best taught backward: cover the keyboard, put the learner's fingers on F and J, and call out letters for them to find blind — starting with home row, one row per day. Correction comes from you watching their fingers, not from them looking at keys.

Whether the learner is a kid or a colleague, the rule holds: their eyes stay on the screen, your eyes stay on their hands. One week of this and the chart is installed for life.

## Typing Finger Chart FAQ

### Which finger types which key?

Pinky-ring-middle-index own successive columns from the edges in; each index finger covers two columns; thumbs own the spacebar. Base position is A-S-D-F / J-K-L-; and every finger returns to home after its reach.

### How long does it take to learn the finger chart?

One focused week of daily 10-minute sessions: two days home row, two top row, two bottom row, one full-chart sentences. Automaticity follows over the next month of use.

### Should I use the same shift key for all capitals?

No — use the opposite-side shift: right-hand letter, left shift; left-hand letter, right shift. Same-side shifting knots the hand and breaks rhythm.

### Is it OK to look at a printed finger chart while learning?

For the first three days, yes — a reference beside the screen is fine. Then remove it: spatial memory only forms when your eyes are denied the answer.
