# Typing Speed by Grade

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/typing-speed-by-grade.html

Grade-level typing expectations are bands, not standards: schools differ enormously in how much keyboarding time they provide. Here is what the published classroom ranges look like, and what to actually teach at each stage.

## The bands

Grade | Typical WPM band | Priority
1st-2nd | 5-11 WPM | Finding letters; keyboard familiarity, not speed
3rd | 10-20 WPM | Home row and correct fingers
4th | 15-25 WPM | All letters without looking down
5th | 25-35 WPM | Accuracy above 90%, capitals with Shift
6th | 30-40 WPM | Punctuation, sustained paragraphs
7th-8th | 35-45 WPM | Editing, formatting, real documents

These are the ranges that appear across classroom keyboarding curricula and district technology plans; treat them as a guide to whether a child is roughly on track, never as a pass or fail line. A child who has had no formal instruction can be two grades behind the band and still catch up in a term.

## Why younger children score lower - and should

Two things are still developing before about age ten: hand size relative to the keyboard, and the fine motor control needed for independent finger movement. A seven-year-old reaching across a full-size keyboard is doing a different physical task from a twelve-year-old.

That is why early keyboarding instruction focuses on letter location and posture rather than words per minute. Pushing speed before the hands are ready produces the two-finger hunt-and-peck style that later caps out around 35-40 WPM.

## What to do at each stage

Grades 1-2: letter games, five minutes at a time, no timer. The goal is knowing where the keys are.
Grades 3-4: home row drills and short un-timed copying. Cover the hands for one round a week once the home row is secure.
Grade 5: timed one-minute tests, but score accuracy first. A child at 90% accuracy will overtake a faster child who types sloppily.
Grades 6-8: full-length tests, real documents, editing practice. This is when speed starts to matter for schoolwork volume.

## How to measure without stressing a child

One test a week, one minute long, and read out the accuracy before the speed. Children optimise for whatever the adult reacts to - if the number that gets attention is WPM, they will type faster and sloppier within a fortnight.

Keep a simple record of the weekly score so progress is visible when it feels slow. Children improve in steps: three flat weeks and then five WPM in one.

## When a child is behind the band

First check the physical setup: feet supported, elbows near 90 degrees, screen at eye level, a keyboard that is not oversized. A child typing on an adult keyboard at the wrong height will underperform regardless of practice.

Then check the method: are they using all fingers and not looking? If not, that is the whole project for the next month, and grade-level speed will follow. If both are fine and the score is flat, the issue is usually practice frequency, not ability - five minutes four times a week beats thirty minutes once.

## Frequently asked questions

### How fast should a 5th grader type?

Roughly 25-35 WPM with 90%+ accuracy if they have had regular keyboarding practice. Bands vary widely by school and by how much instruction the child has actually received.

### How fast should a 3rd grader type?

Usually 10-20 WPM. At this age letter location and home-row habit matter far more than speed, and timed tests should not be the main activity.

### What is a good typing speed for an 8th grader?

35-45 WPM is a reasonable band by the end of middle school, with accuracy above 95%. Students who reach it usually had formal instruction around grade 4 or 5.

### Is 20 WPM bad for a 10-year-old?

No. It is normal for a child without formal keyboarding instruction, and it is well within the grade 4-5 band. What matters is whether the fingers are on the home row.

### How often should children practise?

Ten minutes, three to four times a week. Short frequent sessions build the motor pattern; a long weekly session mostly builds frustration.
