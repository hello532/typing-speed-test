# Transcription Typing Jobs

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/transcription-typing-jobs.html

Transcription typing jobs pay for one thing above all: turning spoken audio into accurate text at speed. Here is what the work really involves, what it takes to qualify, and where the specialisations differ.

## What transcription work actually involves

You listen to audio — interviews, meetings, lectures, dictation — and produce clean, formatted text. The work sounds simple and is not: audio quality varies, speakers overlap, accents differ, and every project has a style guide covering timestamps, speaker labels and how to mark unclear passages. The professionals are not the fastest listeners; they are the most consistent producers of client-ready text.

Pay is usually per audio minute or per project, so your effective hourly rate is speed times accuracy. A typist at 70 WPM with 99% accuracy out-earns a 90 WPM typist who proofreads twice, every time.

## The speed and accuracy reality

The working benchmark is 300+ CPM (60+ WPM) sustained over long audio — not sprint speed. Because audio continues while you type, the real constraint is the listen-type pipeline: holding several seconds of speech in memory while your fingers finish the previous phrase. That skill is trainable, and it improves fastest with actual audio practice rather than plain typing drills.

Accuracy expectations are stricter than almost any other typing job: 99%+ is the professional norm, because errors change meaning. Measure your baseline on the CPM typing test, which shows both CPM and accuracy after every round.

## General, legal and medical transcription

General transcription — podcasts, interviews, lectures — has the lowest barrier: good English, good speed, no special background. Legal transcription adds courtroom and legal-document terminology, formatting conventions and often confidentiality requirements. Medical transcription is the most specialised: clinical vocabulary, drug names and report formats, usually requiring dedicated training and carrying strict privacy obligations.

The pay ladder follows the specialisation: general work is the entry point, and the specialist fields reward the terminology investment. For the medical branch specifically, see medical transcription typing.

## How to qualify, step by step

First, verify you meet the speed floor: 300 CPM at 98%+ accuracy, measured on a three-minute round rather than a sprint. Second, train the audio pipeline: practise typing along with any spoken audio — a podcast at normal speed — starting with pausing freely, then reducing pauses to zero over two weeks. Third, learn a style guide's basics (speaker labels, [unclear] marks, timestamp conventions) because tests grade formatting as well as text.

Fourth, take platform entry tests; they sample exactly these skills. And fifth, build the accuracy rating carefully — in transcription, your rating is your résumé. The typing speed requirements page compares transcription against other typing roles.

## A day in the life of a working transcriptionist

The work arrives as audio files with a deadline and a style guide. A typical rhythm: preview the file for speakers and audio quality, transcribe in 30-45 minute blocks with real breaks between them, then a full proofing pass against the audio before submission. Rush jobs pay premiums; standard jobs pay on accuracy.

What surprises newcomers most: typing is maybe half the time. Listening, researching names and terms, formatting and proofing fill the rest. Effective hourly rate equals audio-minutes-per-hour times rate, which is why the listen-type pipeline matters more than raw WPM.

## Style guides: where tests are actually failed

Every platform and client has a style guide, and entry tests grade it as strictly as the text: how to mark [unclear] audio, whether to clean up false starts, timestamp rules, speaker labels, numbers-as-digits-or-words. Candidates fail tests daily with perfect typing and sloppy formatting.

The fix is unglamorous: read the style guide twice before touching the test audio, and keep it open in a second window while you work. Following a guide exactly is a skill in itself — and it is the one clients mention first in reviews.

## Audio quality, accents and difficult files

Real audio is not studio-clear: background noise, phone recordings, overlapping speakers and strong accents are normal. The professionals' toolkit: good closed-back headphones, a noise-reduction or volume-boost filter in the player, willingness to rewind generously on the first pass, and disciplined [unclear] marking instead of guessing.

Do not cherry-pick easy files forever. Difficult audio is where rates are higher and where your skills actually grow — after the first month, take one hard file for every few comfortable ones.

## Building speed on the listen-type pipeline

The pipeline — hearing ahead while typing behind — has three training stages. Stage one: type along with clear audio, pausing whenever you need. Stage two: no pausing allowed; rewind only at phrase boundaries. Stage three: continuous audio with strategic rewinds, the real working mode.

Two weeks of daily half-hour sessions through these stages transforms throughput more than any keyboard drill. Your raw typing still matters — keep it tuned on the CPM typing test — but the pipeline is where transcription speed actually lives.

## Rates, platforms and when to specialise

General transcription rates sit at the entry level and are volume-driven; legal and medical pay meaningfully more but gate on terminology and, often, certification. The moment to specialise is when your general accuracy rating is consistently high — usually three to six months in — because specialist tests assume your fundamentals are already automatic.

Whichever lane you choose, the economics are identical: effective rate = speed × accuracy × reliability. Speed gets tested once; the other two get reviewed on every single file.

## Transcription Typing Jobs FAQ

### How fast do you need to type for transcription?

300+ CPM (60+ WPM) sustained over long audio, with 99%+ accuracy as the professional norm. Sprint speed matters less than the listen-type pipeline — holding audio in memory while your fingers catch up.

### Is transcription typing hard to learn?

The typing is the easy part if you already meet the speed floor. The learnable skills are the listen-type pipeline, style-guide formatting, and sustained accuracy over long audio — all trainable in weeks with real audio practice.

### What is the difference between general and medical transcription?

General transcription needs only speed and English. Medical transcription adds clinical vocabulary, drug names, report formats and strict privacy obligations — typically requiring dedicated training, and it pays more for it.

### How do I start getting transcription work?

Verify 300 CPM at 98%+ accuracy, train with real audio until you can type without pausing, learn basic style-guide conventions, then take platform entry tests. Your accuracy rating becomes your résumé.
