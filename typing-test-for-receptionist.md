# Typing Test for Receptionist Jobs

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/typing-test-for-receptionist.html

Front-desk work is typing while a phone rings. Employers screen at 40–50 WPM with high accuracy, not at headline speeds. This page covers what each employer type asks for and how to rehearse it.

A receptionist types names, phone numbers, appointment notes and short emails, usually while listening to someone. That means the skill being tested is accurate short-burst typing, not sustained prose speed. Recruiters typically quote 40–50 WPM and an accuracy floor around 95–98%.

The accuracy floor matters more than the speed number: a misspelt surname in a client record is a real cost, whereas 45 WPM instead of 50 is invisible in the day-to-day.

Employer | Typical requirement | What the test adds
Medical or dental front desk | 45–55 WPM, 98% accuracy | Patient names, dates of birth, procedure codes
Hotel or hospitality | 40–45 WPM | Booking references, email addresses
Corporate office | 50 WPM, 97%+ | Minutes, meeting notes, calendar entries
Legal reception | 50–60 WPM, 98%+ | Client names, case references, punctuation
Call centre with reception duties | 35–45 WPM | Typing while talking, call notes

These are the ranges that appear in public job ads and in recruitment guides; treat them as a target band, not a legal threshold. A posting that says 40 WPM rarely rejects a 42 that is clean.

Days 1–5: one 60-second sentence round, three times a day. Type at the speed where you make almost no errors and let the score climb on its own. Most people gain 5–8 WPM in a week at this stage simply by stopping the backspace habit.

Days 6–10: switch to two- and three-minute rounds so you meet the length most screening tests use. Add one Numbers round a day, because phone numbers and reference codes are where front-desk accuracy actually breaks.

Days 11–14: rehearse the real task — read a short message out loud and type it without looking at the keyboard, then check it character by character. That is the job. Then take one five-minute test and record net WPM plus accuracy as the number you quote.

Ask which scoring method the test uses, gross or net, and how long it runs. Then type at 90% of your best pace for the first thirty seconds; almost everyone loses more in corrections than they gain in the opening sprint.

If the test includes punctuation and capitals, do not skip them to save time. Uncorrected omissions are counted as errors on every mainstream test, and they are the most common reason a fast typist fails a receptionist screening.

## Frequently asked questions

### What WPM do receptionist jobs require?

Most postings ask for 40–50 WPM with 95–98% accuracy. Medical and legal front desks sit at the top of that band because names and reference codes must be exact.

### Is 45 WPM fast enough for a receptionist?

Yes for most office, hotel and clinic roles. 45 WPM with clean accuracy clears the common 40 WPM floor; only legal and specialist desks routinely ask for 50–60.

### Do receptionist tests include numbers?

Often. Phone numbers, appointment times and booking references are the realistic content, so practise the Numbers mode as well as sentences. Digits use a different finger pattern.

### How long is a receptionist typing test?

One to three minutes in most screening tools, sometimes five. Train at three minutes so a one-minute test feels short rather than tight.

### Does accuracy matter more than speed?

For this role, yes. Records with wrong digits or misspelt names are rework. A 42 WPM run at 99% is a better signal to an employer than 55 WPM at 92%.
