# Typing Test for Court Clerks

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/typing-test-for-court-clerk.html

A court clerk types case numbers, filing codes, party names and dates into a docket system all day. The speed bar is moderate — 45 to 55 WPM — but an identifier typed wrong creates a filing that cannot be found.

A docket entry looks like this: a case number with a year and a court code, a filing type from a fixed list, the filing party, a date, and a short description. Almost every field is either a code or a proper noun, and several are checked against an existing record.

That shape matters for practice. Copying prose trains sentence rhythm; docket entry trains short alphanumeric bursts with a verification step after each field. The second is what the job actually rewards.

A misspelt word in a note is readable. A case number with a transposed digit is a filing that cannot be retrieved by the parties who need it, and fixing it usually requires a correction entry and a supervisor.

Case numbers - alphanumeric with a year and a court or division code.
Dates - filing date, hearing date, deadline; format depends on the court.
Party names - often unusual spellings, and never autocorrected.
Filing codes - short, similar-looking, and chosen from a list rather than typed freehand.

Type a case number, check it character by character, then type the next one. Ten repetitions of the same number beat one pass through ten different numbers, because the goal is a verification habit rather than exposure to variety.

Then add the field-switching behaviour: case number, Tab, filing type, Tab, date, Enter. Real docket systems move between fields, and typing a clean number followed by a wrong Tab is a lost record just the same.

Band | Clears | Notes
35-45 WPM | Entry-level clerk-typist titles | Usually paired with a data-entry exercise
45-55 WPM | Most court clerk and deputy clerk postings | The common advertised band
55+ WPM | High-volume filing desks | Accuracy floor matters more than the extra speed

Public-sector postings often state the requirement in net WPM on a five-minute sample, and some require a proctored certificate. Check the notice: the wording tells you which document they will accept.

## Frequently asked questions

### What typing speed do court clerks need?

Usually 45-55 WPM with high accuracy, and often on a five-minute proctored sample. The accuracy requirement is strict because a wrong case number creates an unretrievable filing.

### Is court clerk work mostly typing?

A large part of it is docket entry and filing, which is typing in short alphanumeric bursts. The rest is checking documents, answering counter enquiries and managing records.

### Do I need a certificate to apply?

Some jurisdictions require a proctored typing certificate with a stated speed and date; others accept self-certification and test later. The posting states which, and the wording is usually explicit.

### How do I practise case-number accuracy?

Type the same number ten times, checking character by character after each attempt, then move to a new one. Repetition builds the verification habit that prevents transposition errors.

### Does legal terminology matter?

Somewhat. You need to recognise filing types and party designations, but you select codes rather than compose text, so accuracy matters more than vocabulary.
