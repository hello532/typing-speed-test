# Typing Test for Seniors

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/typing-test-for-seniors.html

Typing speed settles with age, but accuracy and comfort do not have to. This page is about testing without strain: longer rounds, bigger text, and a realistic target instead of a leaderboard.

## What actually changes with age

Two things slow down measurably: reaction time and fine motor steadiness. Both show up as more mistyped adjacent keys rather than as slower thinking, which is why older typists often report knowing the word but hitting the wrong key.

What does not change is vocabulary, sentence planning or the knowledge of where the keys are. That is why accuracy-focused practice still pays: the skill you already have is the part that carries over, and the reach is the part that needs the work.

## Set the test up so it measures the right thing

Length: two to three minutes. A 15-second sprint measures reaction time, which is exactly what declines; a longer round measures sustainable pace.
Text: sentences rather than random words. Familiar sentence structure lets you use knowledge instead of reaction.
Display: browser zoom at 125-150% and the widened keyboard view. Reading strain produces more errors than typing strain.
Posture: feet flat, elbows near 90 degrees, screen at eye level. A laptop on a coffee table is the most common cause of shoulder fatigue in an hour of practice.

## A target that is worth having

Thirty to forty words per minute with 97% accuracy covers email, forms, banking, messaging and almost every personal computing task. It is also inside the band that part-time and volunteer clerical roles quote.

Chasing 70 WPM is not useful unless you are going back into transcription or legal support. Chasing 97% accuracy is useful at any age, because it removes the corrections that make typing feel tiring.

## When to stop, and what to watch

Stop a session at the first sign of tingling or numbness in the fingers, or aching in the forearm. Those are early signs of strain and they respond to rest, not to more practice. Persistent numbness is worth a doctor's opinion rather than a typing drill.

Ten minutes, three times a week is a good ceiling to start with. If that feels easy after two weeks, extend the rounds rather than the frequency - shorter sessions recover better.

## Frequently asked questions

### How fast do people over 60 type?

Most sit between 25 and 45 WPM, and the variation within the group is much larger than the average difference from younger typists. Accuracy is usually maintained even when speed settles.

### Is it too late to learn touch typing after 60?

No. Adults learn the key map quickly because they already read and write fluently; the slower part is building new finger habits, which takes a few weeks of short daily practice.

### How long should a test be?

Two to three minutes. Short sprints measure reaction time, which declines with age, so they understate what you can actually sustain.

### Should I use a bigger font?

Yes. Reading strain causes more errors than typing strain at any age. Zoom the browser to 125-150% and use the widened keyboard view.

### What should I do about aching hands?

Stop the session, rest, and check posture and keyboard height before resuming. Persistent numbness or tingling should be reviewed by a doctor rather than worked through.
