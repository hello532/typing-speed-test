# Average Typing Speed by Age

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/average-typing-speed-by-age.html

What is the average typing speed by age? Age sets expectations, but practice sets the number. Here are the benchmarks from kids to seniors — and why the spread inside every age group dwarfs the spread between them.

## The age benchmark table

Typing speed climbs through school years, peaks in young adulthood, and declines slowly — and gently — later in life. These are the commonly observed ranges for each group, measured in net WPM on a standard one-minute test.

Age group | Typical range (WPM) | Notes
Kids (6–11) | 10–20 | Learning letter locations; games help most
Teens (12–17) | 30–45 | Fast learners; chat and gaming drive practice
Young adults (18–30) | 40–55 | Peak years; office and study demands
Adults (31–50) | 40–50 | Stable; technique matters more than age
Seniors (50+) | 25–40 | Gentle decline, offset by experience

## Why the ranges inside each age group are huge

A trained 60-year-old comfortably out-types an untrained 25-year-old. Within every age group, the spread between a hunt-and-peck typist and a touch typist is 3x or more — far larger than the spread between generations. Age predicts opportunity to practice, not ability to type.

That is why comparing yourself to your age band is only useful as a starting point. The meaningful comparison is against your own baseline last month.

## Kids and teens: the practice windows

For kids, short game-style rounds work far better than drills — the typing test for kids is built for exactly that. Teens improve fastest of any group because they already type socially every day; adding ten minutes of deliberate practice to that background volume produces visible gains in weeks.

## Adults and seniors: technique over youth

Adults learning touch typing in their 30s, 40s or beyond reach 40–60 WPM with the same ten-minute daily structure as anyone else — the typing test for adults uses practical, work-oriented texts. Seniors who switch from hunt-and-peck to touch typing typically see the largest relative gains of any group, because they start furthest from their ceiling.

## Where these numbers come from

Age benchmarks are compiled from large volumes of timed typing tests taken by volunteers across schools, offices and online platforms, then grouped by self-reported age. They describe ranges, not destinies: within every band there are six-year-olds who out-type their parents and retirees who out-type their grandchildren.

Two caveats worth keeping in mind. First, self-selected test takers skew slightly faster than the general population. Second, the ranges shift as keyboards arrive earlier in childhood — each generation's teen band has crept upward with device exposure. Use the table as orientation, then measure yourself and work from your own baseline.

## Helping a child learn to type

Children learn fastest through play: short, game-feeling rounds with instant feedback beat formal drills every time. Keep sessions to five or ten minutes, stop before boredom sets in, and praise accuracy over speed — the speed arrives on its own as letter locations become automatic. A physical keyboard sized for small hands helps more than any software feature.

Avoid two traps: letting them hunt-and-peck for months (the habit hardens), and pushing timed tests too early (frustration kills the habit before it forms). Letter familiarity first, gentle timing later.

## Retraining as an adult: what actually changes

Adults retraining from hunt-and-peck face one challenge children do not: unlearning. Your current method is fast enough for daily life, so the temporary slowdown of touch typing feels like a genuine loss. Expect two uncomfortable weeks where you are slower than your old habit — this is the tuition, not a verdict.

The fix is commitment with a boundary: touch type for all practice sessions and all real work for those two weeks, no mixing methods. Adults who hybridise — touch typing for practice, pecking for real email — never consolidate the new skill. Full switch, short discomfort, permanent gain.

## Starting age and the shape of the learning curve

Start at eight and the curve is shallow but long: months of playful familiarity before speed appears, then a steep climb through the teens as motor learning peaks. Start at sixteen and the curve compresses: the same familiarity phase takes weeks, and 40 WPM typically arrives within two to three months of daily practice.

Start at forty or sixty and the curve looks different again: the familiarity phase is similar to a teen's, but the unlearning phase comes first if you hunt-and-peck — two flat weeks where the new method feels slower than the old. After that dip, the climb is the same for everyone. The age you start changes where the curve begins, never how high it goes.

## Age bands in the working world

Employers rarely ask your age when they test your typing — they ask for a number, and the number has no age bracket on it. A 16-year-old and a 60-year-old applying for the same data-entry role face the same KPH bar and the same accuracy cutoff. The age table on this page explains where people typically are; hiring tables describe where they need to be.

The encouraging implication: because practice outweighs age at every stage, the fastest way to beat your age band is the same at 14 and at 64 — ten measured minutes a day. The bands describe populations, not individuals, and individuals always get to choose their side of the curve.

## Average Typing Speed by Age FAQ

### What is the average typing speed for a 12-year-old?

Around 30–45 WPM for teens, though the spread is huge — teens who game and chat daily often out-type adults. Practice frequency matters more than age.

### Does typing speed decline with age?

Gently — typically 10-20% from peak by the senior years, and largely offset by experience. A trained senior comfortably out-types an untrained young adult.

### What is a good typing speed for a kid?

10–20 WPM for ages 6-11 is normal. Short, game-style rounds build speed fastest at that age — accuracy habits matter more than the number.

### Can older adults still learn touch typing?

Yes, and they often see the largest relative gains of any group. The same daily 10-minute structure works at any age — consistency, not youth, is the driver.
