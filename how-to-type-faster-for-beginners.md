# How to Type Faster for Beginners

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/how-to-type-faster-for-beginners.html

How to type faster for beginners comes down to four habits learned in the right order. Skip the hacks — this is the path that actually works, in 10 minutes a day.

## Step one: home row before speed

Every fast typist rests their fingers on the home row: left hand on A-S-D-F, right hand on J-K-L-;, thumbs on space. Every key is reached from there and every finger returns there. It feels slow for about a week — then it becomes automatic and your speed climbs without effort.

Do not practise 'fast' yet. Spend your first sessions on home row typing test drills until you can find every key without looking down. Speed built on top of looking at the keyboard has a hard ceiling; speed built on home row does not.

## Step two: stop looking at the keyboard

Looking down is the single biggest beginner mistake. Every glance breaks your rhythm and costs several words per minute. Cover the keyboard with a light cloth or your hands if you must, and accept the short-term errors — they fade in days, while the habit of looking down lasts years if you let it.

The beginner typing test uses short, friendly texts that make this transition easier. Slow is fine. Eyes up is not negotiable.

## Step three: ten minutes a day, every day

Typing is motor memory, and motor memory grows with frequency, not duration. Ten focused minutes every day beats an hour on weekends. A simple beginner routine: two minutes of home-row warm-up, one full minute measured, five minutes on whatever letters the result flagged as weak, done.

Measure once a week with the same 1 minute typing test and watch the trend, not the daily number. Most beginners double their speed in the first month — from 15–20 WPM to 35–45 — because the early gains are about removing hesitation, not moving faster.

## The five mistakes that slow beginners down

One: practising speed before accuracy — every error costs triple its price in corrections. Two: looking at the keyboard. Three: practising in long, rare sessions. Four: ignoring the weak-key report after each round — it tells you exactly what to fix next. Five: bad posture. Sit up, feet flat, wrists slightly raised; a sore wrist is a slow wrist.

Avoid those five and you will pass the 40 WPM adult average within a few months of daily practice. The learn to type page walks through the full beginner course if you want more structure.

## Your first two weeks, day by day

Days one and two: home row only. Left hand A-S-D-F, right hand J-K-L-;, eyes on the screen no matter what. Type slowly and accept errors — accuracy follows familiarity. Days three and four: add the top row (QWERTY / UIOP), still slow, still eyes up. Days five and six: add the bottom row (ZXCV / BNM) and notice how much of the keyboard you now cover without a glance.

Days seven and eight: first full sentences, at whatever speed keeps you blind. Day nine: first measured minute — record it, whatever it says. Days ten and eleven: numbers and capitals. Days twelve to fourteen: your normal daily structure from this guide, with a weekly retest on day fourteen. Two weeks in, most beginners are typing real text without looking — slowly, but permanently.

## When to move past beginner drills

You are ready to graduate when three things are true at once: you can type a full minute without a single glance at the keyboard, your accuracy holds 95% or better on normal sentences, and your home-row returns are automatic — fingers snap back without thinking. That is usually week three to six of daily practice.

Graduation means changing the stimulus, not abandoning the basics: longer texts, harder text types (numbers, quotes), and the weak-key elimination work from the main system. Keep the eyes-up rule forever; it is the one rule that never stops applying.

## Beginner scenarios: kids, adults and second-language typists

Kids (roughly 8–12): keep sessions to five minutes and game-shaped, stop while it is still fun, and never enforce speed — letter familiarity first, timing later. Adults: expect the two-week unlearning dip where touch typing feels slower than your old habit; switch completely for those weeks, because mixing methods resets the consolidation.

Typing in a second language: your speed will track your reading fluency as much as your finger skill, because look-ahead depends on recognising words before you type them. Practise with the most common words of the target language first — the vocabulary-driven speed comes faster than most people expect, and it transfers back to first-language typing as cleaner accuracy habits.

## What to do when practice feels boring

Every beginner hits the week-two wall where drills feel repetitive and the keyboard feels like homework. It passes, but only if you change the text, not the habit. Rotate what you type: song lyrics you like, a paragraph from a book you are reading, yesterday's email draft retyped. The skill being trained — accuracy, rhythm, blind reach — does not care about the content.

The other boredom killer is visible progress. Watch your own weekly number for three weeks and the routine stops feeling like a chore, because it starts reading like a score you are beating. Boredom is almost never about typing; it is about practising without evidence that it is working.

## How to Type Faster for Beginners FAQ

### How long does it take a beginner to type faster?

Most beginners double their speed in the first month of daily 10-minute practice — typically from 15–20 WPM to 35–45 WPM. Early gains come from removing hesitation, not moving faster.

### Should beginners learn home row first?

Yes. Home row (ASDF / JKL;) is the foundation of touch typing. Speed built without it has a hard ceiling; speed built on it compounds automatically.

### Is it OK to look at the keyboard sometimes?

No — every glance breaks rhythm and reinforces the habit. Cover the keyboard for a few days if needed. Short-term errors fade fast; the looking-down habit lasts years.

### What should a beginner practice first?

Home row drills until you can find keys blind, then short daily rounds on a beginner typing test with a weekly 1 minute typing test to track progress.
