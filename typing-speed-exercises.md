# Typing Speed Exercises

Source: https://typing.rerivo.com/typing-speed-exercises.html

The best typing speed exercises share one trait: each targets a specific bottleneck. These eight drills, used in a 10-minute daily structure, cover every bottleneck a typist has.

## Sprint drills for raw ceiling

Two or three 15-second rounds at absolute maximum effort, accuracy be damned. Sprints expand your speed ceiling the way interval training expands a runner's — you are teaching your fingers that a higher gear exists. Follow each sprint with a slow, clean round so the sloppiness does not stick.

Use the typing trainer for these, and note your sprint WPM separately. The gap between sprint speed and working speed is your available headroom.

## Weak-key elimination

After any round, the result panel lists your most-missed and slowest keys. Take the two worst and drill them in 15-second bursts until they stop appearing in the report — then take the next two. This is the highest-ROI exercise in typing, because one hesitant key taxes every word that contains it.

Most people discover a pattern: a specific finger, a specific row, or a specific symbol family. Fix the pattern, not just the key. When the pattern is a letter pair rather than a finger, bigram typing practice drills those combinations directly.

## Symbol and number rounds

Digits and punctuation break more rhythms than letters do, because everyday typing barely uses them. Two rounds a week in numbers or quotes mode keeps them from ambushing you in real documents. If your work is symbol-heavy — code, spreadsheets, chat with emoji — make it three rounds.

The pangram typing test complements this by forcing all 26 letters into a single sentence, including the rare q, z, x and j that normal prose skips.

## Endurance and rhythm sets

Once a week, take a three- or five-minute round and watch how your speed and accuracy decay. Early fatigue shows up as errors in the final third. Endurance is trainable: the decay curve flattens noticeably within a month of weekly long rounds.

Rhythm work pairs with it: type to a steady internal count rather than in bursts and stalls. Consistency above 85% on the result panel is the marker of a typist whose speed will hold on long documents.

## The daily 10-minute structure

Put it together: two sprint rounds as warm-up (1 min), one full measured minute, five minutes on your two weakest keys, two minutes of a text type you find hard — numbers, quotes, or quotes for punctuation. Ten minutes, every day, beats any weekend marathon. Measure weekly, adjust drills to whatever the report flags, and repeat.

## How to know which drill you need today

The result panel answers this if you read it in the right order. Net WPM much lower than raw WPM means errors are your bottleneck: accuracy rounds and weak-key elimination. High accuracy but low consistency means rhythm is the problem: endurance sets and steady-count rounds. Good scores on prose but collapses on numbers or punctuation means symbols: dedicated numbers and quotes rounds.

Flat scores for two or more weeks despite daily practice means plateau: change the stimulus entirely — new text type, different duration, or a drill you have never done. The exercise menu is large on purpose; the skill is matching it to the current bottleneck.

## A sample weekly exercise schedule

Monday: sprints and weak keys (the classic ten-minute structure). Tuesday: numbers or quotes mode, accuracy focus. Wednesday: one measured minute plus weak-key work. Thursday: rest or light home-row review — adaptation needs recovery. Friday: sprints and a hard text type. Saturday: the weekly long round — three to five minutes, watching the decay curve. Sunday: weekly retest and five-minute review of the trend.

The schedule is a template, not a rule. The fixed points are daily short practice, one long round per week, and one honest measurement. Everything else rotates around whatever the result panel flagged most recently.

## Exercise mistakes that waste your practice time

The most common waste is drilling strength instead of weakness: re-running full tests because they feel productive, while the weak-key report gathers dust. A full test tells you what is wrong; only targeted drills fix it. Second: changing exercises daily. A drill needs about a week of repetition to move its bottleneck — rotating daily means nothing ever consolidates.

Third: practising tired. Fatigue converts directly into errors, and errors rehearsed become habits. End the session when accuracy degrades, not when the clock says so. Finally: measuring progress by effort ('I practised an hour') instead of by the weekly number. Effort is not the metric; the trend line is.

## Building custom drills from your weak-key data

The most powerful exercise is the one you build yourself. Take your three weakest keys from the result panel and write — or generate with the custom text box — a short text that overloads them: a sentence packed with those letters, repeated in different word shapes. Typing ' jazz quiz zigzag ' twenty times fixes a weak z faster than any generic drill, because the repetition is concentrated exactly on your deficit.

Refresh the custom drill weekly as the report changes. Weak keys migrate: fix q and the bottleneck moves to punctuation, then to digits. The drill that follows the data is the drill that keeps working.

One final rule ties all eight drills together: finish every session with one clean, measured round. It cements the day's accuracy before you stop, and it leaves a comparable number in your history for the weekly review. Drills change the bottleneck; the closing round proves it.

## Typing Speed Exercises FAQ

### What is the best typing exercise for speed?

15-second maximum-effort sprints for ceiling, plus daily weak-key elimination for the biggest single gains. One hesitant key taxes every word containing it.

### How often should I do typing exercises?

Ten minutes daily. Motor memory consolidates between sessions, so daily short practice beats long weekend sessions every time.

### Do I need special exercises for numbers and symbols?

Yes — two rounds a week in numbers or quotes mode. Symbols are the most common rhythm-breaker because everyday typing barely uses them.

### How do I stop my speed dropping on long texts?

Weekly 3-5 minute endurance rounds. The decay curve flattens within a month, and rhythm training (steady internal count) keeps accuracy stable to the end.
